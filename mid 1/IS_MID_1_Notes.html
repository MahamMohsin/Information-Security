Official Definition

Computer Security Defined (NISTIR 7298)

The standard reference definition adopted in this course is drawn from the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) Interagency Report NISTIR 7298 (Glossary of Key Information Security Terms, May 2013):

NISTIR 7298 Definition
"Computer Security: Measures and controls that ensure confidentiality, integrity, and availability of information system assets including hardware, software, firmware, and information being processed, stored, and communicated."

This definition establishes the baseline: security is not merely software features, but comprehensive measures and controls safeguarding assets across all three operational states: processing, storage, and transmission/communication.

Core Framework

The CIA Triad & The Two Extended Objectives

Computer security rests on three foundational pillars known as the CIA Triad, augmented by two essential extended concepts required for complete operational security:

🔒

1. Confidentiality

Preserving authorized restrictions on information access and disclosure, including means for protecting personal privacy and proprietary information.

Data Confidentiality: Assures that private or confidential information is not made available or disclosed to unauthorized individuals.
Privacy: Assures that individuals control or influence what information related to them may be collected and stored, by whom, and to whom it may be disclosed.
Loss: Unauthorized disclosure of information.
🛡️

2. Integrity

Guarding against improper information modification or destruction, including ensuring information nonrepudiation and authenticity.

Data Integrity: Assures that information and programs are changed only in a specified and authorized manner.
System Integrity: Assures that a system performs its intended function in an unimpaired manner, free from deliberate or inadvertent unauthorized manipulation.
Loss: Unauthorized modification or destruction of information.
⚡

3. Availability

Ensuring timely and reliable access to and use of information and information systems.

Operational Target: Assures that systems work promptly and service is not denied to authorized users when needed.
Criticality: The more critical a service or component, the higher the required availability level.
Loss: Disruption of access to or use of information or an information system.
📜

4. Authenticity (Extended Concept)

The property of being genuine and being able to be verified and trusted; confidence in the validity of a transmission, a message, or message originator.

Operational Meaning: Verifying that users are who they say they are, and that each input arriving at the system came from a trusted source.
🔍

5. Accountability (Extended Concept)

The security goal that generates the requirement for actions of an entity to be traced uniquely to that entity.

Operational Functions: Supports nonrepudiation, deterrence, fault isolation, intrusion detection and prevention, and after-action recovery and legal action. Systems must maintain audit logs for forensic analysis and transaction disputes.

FIPS 199 Standard

FIPS 199 Impact Levels & Authoritative Slide Examples

FIPS 199 (Standards for Security Categorization of Federal Information and Information Systems) categorizes the impact of security breaches on organizational operations, assets, or individuals into three distinct levels:

Impact Level Official Definition (FIPS 199) Magnitude of Harm
LOW IMPACT The loss of confidentiality, integrity, or availability could be expected to have a limited adverse effect on organizational operations, organizational assets, or individuals. Causes minor degradation in mission capability; minor damage to assets; minor financial loss; minor harm to individuals.
MODERATE IMPACT The loss of confidentiality, integrity, or availability could be expected to have a serious adverse effect on organizational operations, organizational assets, or individuals. Causes significant degradation in mission capability; significant damage to assets; significant financial loss; significant harm (non-fatal) to individuals.
HIGH IMPACT The loss of confidentiality, integrity, or availability could be expected to have a severe or catastrophic adverse effect on organizational operations, organizational assets, or individuals. Causes severe degradation or inability to perform mission functions; major damage to assets; severe financial loss; severe injury or loss of human life.

Theoretical Analysis

The 10 Challenges of Computer Security

Computer security is inherently complex and counterintuitive. The lecture slides detail 10 fundamental challenges:

01
Deceptive Simplicity vs Implementation Complexity

Requirements seem straightforward and can be described with self-explanatory one-word labels (confidentiality, authentication, nonrepudiation, integrity). However, the internal mechanisms meeting these requirements are highly complex and demand subtle mathematical reasoning.

02
Unorthodox Adversary Thinking

In developing defenses, designers must anticipate attacks. Attackers succeed by looking at the problem from completely novel angles, exploiting unexpected weaknesses rather than attacking defenses frontally.

03
Counterintuitive Procedures

Procedures required for security often appear illogical or excessively complex to a novice. Elaborate measures only make logical sense when every subtle aspect of the threat model is fully analyzed.

04
Physical and Logical Placement Dilemmas

Designers must decide where mechanisms should reside both physically (specific network nodes/perimeters) and logically (which layer of the TCP/IP architectural stack).

05
Secret Management & Protocol Dependency

Security mechanisms require participants to possess secret information (e.g., cryptographic keys), introducing difficult challenges in generation, distribution, and storage. Furthermore, reliance on underlying communication protocols (which may introduce variable network delays) can disrupt time-critical security checks.

06
Asymmetric Battle of Wits (The Attacker's Asymmetry)

A continuous battle of wits between the attacker and defender. The attacker has a supreme structural advantage: the attacker needs to discover only a single vulnerability, whereas the designer must anticipate and eliminate all vulnerabilities to achieve security.

07
Invisible Return on Investment

There is a natural tendency for users and executives to perceive little benefit from security expenditures until a catastrophic security failure occurs.

08
Constant Monitoring in Overloaded Environments

Security requires continuous, regular surveillance and vigilance, which is extremely difficult in modern fast-paced, resource-constrained operational settings.

09
Security as an Afterthought (Bolted-on vs Built-in)

Security is still too often tacked onto an existing system after implementation is complete, rather than being engineered as an integral part of the initial architecture.

10
Perceived Impediment to Usability

Users and administrators often view robust security mechanisms as frustrating impediments to user-friendly operation, productivity, and system efficiency.

Subfields & Dimensions of Cybersecurity

As presented in Slide 11, organizational cybersecurity spans multiple interrelated operational domains:

  • Information Security (InfoSec): Designed to ensure that only authorized users, apps, and systems access sensitive information.
  • Network Security: Measures such as encryption, traffic monitoring, firewalls, VPNs, and endpoint controls ensure data integrity and confidentiality across distributed networks.
  • Infrastructure / Hardware Security: Physical CPU, server chassis, and data center hardware protections.
  • Cloud Security: Safeguards cloud infrastructure and multi-tenant data stores against evolving cyber threats.
  • Application & OS Security: Eliminates software coding flaws and operating system privilege escalation paths.
  • Organizational Policy Framework: The governance structure responsible for formally mitigating institutional security risks.
  • End-User Behavior & Endpoint Security: End-users represent the first line of defense; human factor vulnerabilities must be controlled, and endpoint devices (laptops, phones, tablets) hardened.

Assets & The 3 Vulnerability Classes (NRC02)

4 Categories of Information System Assets

An asset is any system resource that has value to the organization and requires protection:

💻
1. Hardware

Computers, servers, processing units, storage devices, data center facilities, and perimeter controllers.

⚙️
2. Software

Operating systems, system utilities, middleware, database management systems, and business application programs.

📁
3. Data

Files, databases, credentials, configuration profiles, intellectual property, and transient processed records.

🌐
4. Communication Facilities

Routers, switches, communication links, bridges, firewalls, and interconnected network channels.

According to the National Research Council report (NRC02) cited in the slides, all system vulnerabilities can be grouped into three fundamental classes that correspond directly to the CIA objectives:

Corrupted Vulnerability

Corrupted

System resources or data are modified in an unauthorized or unintended manner.

Maps to: Loss of Integrity
Leaky Vulnerability

Leaky

Maps to: Loss of Confidentiality
Unavailable Vulnerability

Unavailable / Very Slow

System resources become unresponsive, denied, or severely degraded in service.

Maps to: Loss of Availability
Attack Taxonomy

Threat Agents, Attacks & Countermeasures

Threat: A potential security harm to an asset. It represents a circumstance or event capable of exploiting a vulnerability.
Attack: A threat that is actively carried out (threat action). When successful, it produces an undesirable violation of security (threat consequence).
Threat Agent: The entity or adversary (attacker) carrying out the attack.

Classification of Attacks

By Nature of Action

  • Active Attack: An attempt to alter system resources or affect their operation.
    Impact: Threat to Integrity and Availability (e.g., DoS attack, grade tampering).
  • Passive Attack: An attempt to learn or make use of information from the system without affecting system resources.
    Impact: Threat to Confidentiality (e.g., packet sniffing, wiretapping, traffic pattern analysis).

By Origin / Perimeter

  • Inside Attack: Initiated by an entity inside the security perimeter (an "insider"). The insider possesses legitimate authorization to access resources, but utilizes them in ways unauthorized by the administrators.
  • Outside Attack: Initiated from outside the perimeter by an unauthorized/illegitimate entity. Ranges from amateur script pranksters to organized criminals, terrorists, and hostile nation-states.

Countermeasures & Residual Risk

A countermeasure is any means taken to deal with a security attack. Slide 23 details the lifecycle and constraints:

Phase 1
Prevention (Ideal)

Devise controls to prevent a particular type of attack from succeeding entirely.

Phase 2
Detection

When prevention fails or is unfeasible, detect the occurrence of the attack promptly.

Phase 3
Recovery

Recover system state, restore data integrity, and isolate damaged components.

The Law of Residual Risk
Countermeasures may themselves introduce new vulnerabilities. Residual vulnerabilities invariably remain after imposing countermeasures, leaving a residual level of risk to organizational assets. Owners must minimize that risk given budget and operational constraints.

Core Principles

The 13 Fundamental Security Design Principles

Slides 3 and 4 present 13 foundational engineering design principles for secure computer systems, along with explicit concrete examples that must be memorized for MID-1:

01

Economy of Mechanism

Definition: Keep security mechanisms as simple and straightforward as possible. This reduces the potential for errors, oversights, and vulnerabilities that inherently arise from complex designs.

Slide Example: Avoiding convoluted permission logic; simplicity makes formal analysis and verification feasible.
02

Fail-Safe Default

Definition: The default action should always be to deny access or secure access. Access decisions must be based on explicit permission rather than exclusion.

Slide Example: If a network firewall crashes or fails, its default state must be to let no traffic in (fail closed), rather than opening the perimeter.
03

Complete Mediation

Definition: Ensure that every single access to every resource is checked for authorization; access decisions must not rely on stale cached permissions.

Slide Example: If a user changes their password or session context, the system re-prompts for full authentication rather than relying on previous cached authorization.
04

Open Design

Definition: The design of a security mechanism should be completely open rather than secret (rejection of "security through obscurity").

Slide Example: Although cryptographic encryption keys must remain secret, the mathematical encryption algorithms themselves (e.g., DES, AES) must be open to public scrutiny and rigorous cryptanalysis.
05

Separation of Privilege

Definition: Multiple privilege attributes are required to achieve access to a restricted resource. Access should never be granted based on a single condition or key.

Slide Example: Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) — requiring both something the user knows (password) and something the user has (smart card / hardware token) to authorize entry.
06

Least Privilege

Definition: Grant users, processes, and applications only the absolute minimum permissions strictly necessary to perform their legitimate operational tasks.

Slide Example: Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) model, where privileges are bounded strictly to specific job roles rather than granting full administrative root access.
07

Least Common Mechanism

Definition: Sharing components or resources among different users or processes should be minimized as much as possible. This minimizes the potential for one user's actions to compromise another user's security.

Slide Example: In memory and execution stacks, if a user shares a file within a user module, the shared resource must be strictly isolated inside that user module rather than accessible globally across kernel space.
08

Psychological Acceptability

Definition: Security measures must be designed in a way that introduces minimum friction and hurdles to the legitimate user of the system.

Slide Example: When a person enters campus, using a rapid seamless eye/iris scan rather than forcing them to locate, swipe, and verify a physical plastic access card.
09

Isolation

Definition: Systems, processes, or resources containing critical data must be isolated to strictly restrict public access and prevent cross-boundary lateral movement.

Slide Example: Multi-user operating systems where each user account runs isolated from all others, or running untrusted environments inside isolated Virtual Machines (VMs).
10

Encapsulation

Definition: A specific form of isolation based on object-oriented programming functionality, where internal state is protected behind defined interface boundaries.

Slide Example: When designing a password verification module, all hashing algorithms and credential management methods are bundled inside a protected class with private members, inaccessible from outside code.
11

Modularity

Definition: Security mechanisms must be constructed as separate, autonomous, and protected modules using a modular architecture.

Slide Example: If an encryption library or authentication routine requires a security patch, only that specific isolated module is updated without destabilizing or recompiling the entire host system.
12

Layering (Defense in Depth)

Definition: The use of multiple, overlapping protection approaches addressing people, technology, and operational aspects of information systems.

Slide Example: Presenting an attacker with multiple concentric barriers: user category role checks, biometric identification, perimeter network firewalls, and storage encryption.
13

Least Astonishment

Definition: The user interface and security behavior of the system must not amaze, confuse, or surprise the user while accessing the secure system; controls must align with user mental models.

Symmetric Cipher Model & The 5 Ingredients (Figure 2.1)

Symmetric encryption (also termed conventional encryption, single-key encryption, or secret-key encryption) remains the workhorse of bulk data protection. It uses a single shared secret key for both encryption and decryption.

Plaintext

The original intelligible message or data fed into the algorithm as input.

2

Performs various mathematical substitutions and transformations on the plaintext.

3
Secret Key

Exact substitutions and transformations depend strictly on this input key.

4
Ciphertext

The scrambled message produced as output. Two different keys yield two different ciphertexts.

5
Decryption Algorithm

The encryption algorithm run in reverse. Takes ciphertext and secret key to recreate original plaintext.

Mandatory Requirements

The 2 Requirements for Secure Symmetric Encryption (Slide 16)

According to Slide 16, two rigorous conditions must be satisfied for secure deployment:

💪

Requirement 1: Strong Encryption Algorithm

At a minimum, an opponent who knows the algorithm and has access to one or more ciphertexts must be unable to decipher the ciphertext or figure out the key.

The opponent should be unable to decrypt ciphertext or discover the key even if they possess a number of ciphertexts together with the matching plaintexts that produced each ciphertext (known-plaintext security).
🔑

Sender and receiver must obtain copies of the secret key in a secure fashion and must keep the key strictly secure.

Catastrophic Exposure Rule
If someone can discover the secret key and knows the algorithm, all communications encrypted with that key become completely readable.
Attack Analysis

Brute-Force Attacks & Exhaustive Key Search (Table 2.2)

An adversary can attack a symmetric encryption scheme via two fundamental approaches: Brute-Force or Cryptanalysis.

Brute-Force Mathematics & Conditions (Slides 17–18)

  • Mechanism: Trying every possible key on a sample of ciphertext until an intelligible plaintext translation is obtained.
  • Average Effort: On average, half of all possible keys must be tried to achieve success. For a key space of size $x = 2^k$, the average tries required is $x/2 = 2^{k-1}$.
  • Key Size Impact: The key length directly determines brute-force resistance. Larger keys exponentially expand search duration.
  • Plaintext Recognition Factors:
    • Known Language: Plaintext in a known natural language makes recognition easy.
    • Compression: If text was compressed prior to encryption, recognition becomes significantly more difficult.
    • Compressed Numerical Data: If the file consists of compressed numerical data, recognizing valid plaintext becomes extremely difficult.

Analytical Attack

Cryptanalysis (Slide 19)

Cryptanalysis refers to the process of analyzing information systems in order to understand hidden aspects of the systems without possessing the secret key.

Operational Basis

Cryptanalytic attacks rely on:

  • The mathematical nature of the algorithm.
  • Knowledge of general characteristics of the plaintext (e.g., character frequency, file headers).
  • Knowledge of sample plaintext-ciphertext pairs.
Catastrophic Consequence

Cryptanalysis attempts to deduce a specific plaintext or deduce the secret key being used. If an attack succeeds in deducing the key, the effect is catastrophic: all past and future messages encrypted with that key are completely compromised.

Block Ciphers

Processes the plaintext input in fixed-size blocks (e.g., 64 bits for DES, 128 bits for AES) and produces a block of ciphertext of equal size for each plaintext block. Longer plaintexts are processed as a series of sequential blocks.

Encrypts continuous streams of data, processing 1 bit or 1 byte at a time. Uses a pseudorandom byte generator (keystream generator) whose output byte stream is combined (XORed) with plaintext bytes.

Slide Applications: Data communications channels, browser/web links (TLS/SSL).

Theoretical Foundations

Feistel Cipher Structure & Product Ciphers

In 1949, Claude Shannon published the seminal paper "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems", introducing the concept of product ciphers that alternate between substitution and permutation to thwart statistical cryptanalysis. In 1973, Horst Feistel of IBM devised the practical architectural implementation now known as the Feistel Cipher Structure (Figure 20.1 / Slide 36), which serves as the structural foundation of the Data Encryption Standard (DES).

Shannon's Two Core Building Blocks: Confusion & Diffusion (Slide 51)

🌪️

1. Diffusion

Definition: The statistical structure of the plaintext is dissipated into long-range statistics of the ciphertext. Each plaintext digit affects the value of many ciphertext digits, and each ciphertext digit is affected by many plaintext digits.

Mechanism: Achieved through Permutations (P-boxes) and bit transpositions.
Objective: Makes the statistical relationship between plaintext and ciphertext as complex as possible to thwart statistical frequency analysis.
🧩

2. Confusion

Definition: Seeks to make the relationship between the statistics of the ciphertext and the value of the encryption key as complex as possible.

Mechanism: Achieved through complex non-linear Substitutions (S-boxes).
Objective: Even if an adversary discovers statistical patterns in ciphertext, the link to the secret key remains mathematically intractable. A simple linear substitution adds little confusion.
Avalanche Effect Slide Example (Slide 51)
Consider two plaintext blocks differing by only 1 single bit:
V1 = 0010 1011
V2 = 0010 0011 (bit 5 flipped from 1 to 0)

After round transformations, the ciphertexts produce:
y1 = 1011 1001
y2 = 0110 1100 (6 out of 8 bits flipped!)

"Confusion is apparently 'enough' since One-Time Pad (OTP) is secure. But diffusion alone is perhaps not enough at least using relatively small blocks."

Reversible vs. Irreversible Transformations (Slide 34–36)

A critical engineering elegance of the Feistel network is that the round function $F$ does NOT need to be invertible:

Component Invertibility Requirement Mathematical Rationale in Feistel Design
Overall Cipher MUST BE REVERSIBLE Decryption must recover the exact original plaintext $X$ from ciphertext $Y$.
Initial & Final Permutations (IP / FP) REVERSIBLE $FP = IP^{-1}$. One is the exact inverse table of the other.
Round Function $F$ (S-Boxes, Expansion) DOES NOT REQUIRE INVERTIBILITY The function $F$ is never inverted! During decryption, $F(R, K)$ is computed in the forward direction and XORed with the opposite half: $(L \oplus F(R, K)) \oplus F(R, K) = L$. S-boxes can be completely non-invertible many-to-one mappings.

Feistel Cipher Design Parameters (Slides 53–54)

Block Size

Larger block size increases security via greater diffusion, but reduces execution speed. 64-bit was traditional (DES); 128-bit is modern (AES).

Key Size

Larger key size increases security via greater resistance to brute-force and greater confusion, but may reduce speed. 128-bit key is now standard.

Number of Rounds

A single round offers inadequate security; multiple rounds offer increasing security. Standard typical configuration is 16 rounds.

Subkey Generation Algorithm

Greater mathematical complexity in round key derivation increases resistance to cryptanalysis.

Round Function $F$

Greater complexity (S-boxes, non-linear mixing) provides greater resistance to advanced cryptanalysis.

Fast Software Execution & Ease of Analysis

Needs efficient execution in software applications; clarity in design allows rigorous verification of security assurance.

Worked Solution

Worked Proof: Feistel Decryption Reversal (Slide 55 & Figure 4.4)

Step 1: Given Conditions

Consider a 2-round Feistel system. Plaintext is split into 32-bit halves $(LE_0, RE_0)$. Encryption executes 16 rounds producing output $(LE_{16}, RE_{16})$. Before passing to the inverse permutation, the halves are swapped to form the preoutput ciphertext:

$$LD_0 = RE_{16}, \quad RD_0 = LE_{16}$$
Step 2: Encryption Round Formula

For any encryption round $i$ ($1 \le i \le 16$):

$$LE_i = RE_{i-1}$$
$$RE_i = LE_{i-1} \oplus F(RE_{i-1}, K_i)$$
Step 3: Decryption Round Formula

Decryption feeds the swapped preoutput $(LD_0, RD_0)$ into the identical Feistel structure using subkeys in reverse order ($K_{16}, K_{15}, \dots, K_1$):

$$LD_i = RD_{i-1}$$
$$RD_i = LD_{i-1} \oplus F(RD_{i-1}, K_{16-i+1})$$
Step 4: Algebraic Proof of Round 1 Decryption

For round 1 of decryption using subkey $K_{16}$:

$$LD_1 = RD_0 = LE_{16} = RE_{15}$$
$$egin{aligned} RD_1 &= LD_0 \oplus F(RD_0, K_{16}) \ &= RE_{16} \oplus F(LE_{16}, K_{16}) \ &= [LE_{15} \oplus F(RE_{15}, K_{16})] \oplus F(RE_{15}, K_{16}) \end{aligned}$$

By the associative and self-inverting property of XOR ($A \oplus B \oplus B = A \oplus 0 = A$):

$$RD_1 = LE_{15}$$
Step 5: General Induction & Final Answer

By mathematical induction, after $j$ rounds of decryption:

$$LD_j = RE_{16-j}, \quad RD_j = LE_{16-j}$$

After round 16 using $K_1$:

$$LD_{16} = RE_0, \quad RD_{16} = LE_0$$

A final 32-bit swap yields $(LE_0, RE_0)$, exactly reconstructing the original 64-bit plaintext!

Conclusion: Decryption requires no inverted functions and runs the identical hardware circuit, needing only the reverse ordering of the 16 subkeys!

Key Schedule

DES Key Generation Schedule (Figure 6.10 & Slides 42–46)

The round-key generator creates sixteen 48-bit subkeys out of a 56-bit cipher key (derived from a 64-bit key with parity bits):

1️⃣

Parity Drop (PC-1)

64-bit key contains 8 parity bits (bits 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64) where each parity bit is the XOR sum of the preceding 7 bits. Permuted Choice 1 (PC-1) drops these 8 bits, outputting 56 bits.

2️⃣

Circular Left Shifts

The 56 bits are split into two 28-bit halves ($C_0, D_0$). In each round, both halves are shifted left circularly by either 1 or 2 bit positions:

Shift Schedule:
• Rounds 1, 2, 9, 16: 1 bit shift
• All other 12 rounds: 2 bits shift
3️⃣

Compression P-Box (PC-2)

After shifting, the 56 bits pass through Permuted Choice 2 (PC-2). PC-2 permutes the bits and drops 8 specific bits to output a unique 48-bit subkey $K_i$ for that round.

Why 16 Rounds? The 28-Bit Shift Restoration Proof (Slide 56)
Two critical reasons justify the choice of exactly 16 rounds in DES:
  1. Scrambling Requirement: A minimum of 12 rounds were needed to sufficiently scramble the key and data together to guarantee full confusion and diffusion.
  2. Key Restoration Property: The total circular shifts across all 16 rounds sum exactly to:
    $$ ext{Total Shifts} = (4 imes 1 ext{ bit}) + (12 imes 2 ext{ bits}) = 4 + 24 = \mathbf{28 ext{ bits}}$$
    Because each key half is exactly 28 bits long, shifting circularly by 28 bits completes a full cycle ($28 \pmod{28} = 0$), returning the key halves back to their exact original starting positions inside hardware registers for the next encryption block!

Transformation Pipeline

The Four AES Round Transformations (Figure 7.5 & Slides 11–18)

Every standard AES round executes four operations: one permutation (ShiftRows) and three substitutions (SubBytes, MixColumns, AddRoundKey):

Figure 7.5 Structure of Each Round at Encryption Site
Figure 7.5: Dataflow of an AES round. Note 1: An initial AddRoundKey is executed before Round 1. Note 2: MixColumns is omitted in the final round.

1. SubBytes (Byte Substitution)

Non-linear byte substitution operating independently on all 16 bytes using a $16 imes 16$ S-box matrix containing all 256 possible 8-bit values ($2^8 = 256$).

Lookup Rule: The byte $\{xy\}$ is treated as two hexadecimal digits. Row $= x$ (left digit), Column $= y$ (right digit). The intersection $S_{x,y}$ replaces the byte. Invertible via InvSubBytes.

2. ShiftRows (Row Permutation)

A byte-level permutation that permutes bytes across columns by performing a circular left shift on each row of the State matrix:

  • Row 0: No shift (0 bytes shifted)
  • Row 1: Circular left shift by 1 byte
  • Row 2: Circular left shift by 2 bytes
  • Row 3: Circular left shift by 3 bytes
Invertible via InvShiftRows (circular right shifts by 1, 2, 3 bytes).

3. MixColumns (Column Diffusion)

Operates column by column. Each column of 4 bytes is treated as a 4-term polynomial over $ ext{GF}(2^8)$ and multiplied modulo $(x^4 + 1)$ with a constant matrix:

$$egin{bmatrix} s'_{0,j} \ s'_{1,j} \ s'_{2,j} \ s'_{3,j} \end{bmatrix} = egin{bmatrix} 02 & 03 & 01 & 01 \ 01 & 02 & 03 & 01 \ 01 & 01 & 02 & 03 \ 03 & 01 & 01 & 02 \end{bmatrix} egin{bmatrix} s_{0,j} \ s_{1,j} \ s_{2,j} \ s_{3,j} \end{bmatrix}$$
Formula for Row 0 (Slide 17):
$s'_{0,j} = (02 \cdot s_{0,j}) \oplus (03 \cdot s_{1,j}) \oplus s_{2,j} \oplus s_{3,j}$
Multiplications and additions are strictly in $ ext{GF}(2^8)$ arithmetic. Omitted in final round.

4. AddRoundKey & Key Expansion

A simple bitwise XOR combining the 128 bits of the current State matrix with a 128-bit round key (4 words):

$$ ext{State}' = ext{State} \oplus ext{RoundKey}$$
Key Expansion (Slide 18): Takes the 4-word (16-byte) master key and produces 44 words (176 bytes). This provides 4 words for the initial pre-round transformation and 4 words for each of the 10 rounds!
Inverse Structure

AES Decryption & Inverse Ciphers (Figure 7.17)

Because AES is not a Feistel cipher, decryption does NOT execute the same forward transformations. Decryption requires explicit inverse operations run in reverse order:

  • InvSubBytes: Inverts S-box substitutions using inverse S-box.
  • InvShiftRows: Performs circular right shifts (Row 1 by 1, Row 2 by 2, Row 3 by 3 bytes).
  • InvMixColumns: Multiplies columns by the inverse polynomial matrix in $ ext{GF}(2^8)$.
  • AddRoundKey: Identical to encryption (since XOR is its own mathematical inverse: $A \oplus K \oplus K = A$).
Diffusion Property

Avalanche Effect in AES (Slide 19)

Claude Shannon (1949) specified that a robust cipher must exhibit strong diffusion and confusion. In AES, changing a single bit in the plaintext produces radical changes across nearly all ciphertext bytes after just a few rounds:

Plaintext 1: 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00
Plaintext 2: 01 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 (1 bit difference)

Ciphertext 1: 63 26 9B BC A1 56 ED 0F 04 01 30 63 92 73 13 42
Ciphertext 2: C0 17 48 29 8B 1F ED 99 22 55 1B 89 05 3C 60 7E (Over 50% of bits flipped!)

Confusion = Substitution (byte-by-byte via S-Box); Diffusion = Permutation & Mixing (ShiftRows + MixColumns).

High-Yield Exam Rules
  • Non-Feistel: AES processes all 128 bits in parallel across Galois Fields ($ ext{GF}(2^8)$).
  • Parameters: 128b key → 10 rounds; 192b → 12 rounds; 256b → 14 rounds. Round keys are ALWAYS 128b.

Q: Why is MixColumns omitted in the final round of AES?

A: Omitting MixColumns in the final round makes the encryption and decryption structures symmetrical, allowing the inverse cipher to execute with lower hardware overhead.

Message Authentication & Cryptographic Hash Functions

Lectures: 7, 8, 9 Primitives: MAC & Secure Hash Functions (SHA) Slides: Week 3 (21–35)
Authentication Fundamentals

Message Authentication & Authentication Without Confidentiality

Message authentication is a mechanism or service used to verify the integrity of a message and ensure that the transmitting entity is the authentic originator. It applies to both transmitted network packets and stored static files (data authentication).

Authentication via Symmetric Encryption (Slide 22)

If sender and receiver share a secret key $K$, only the genuine sender can encrypt a message that decrypts intelligibly. When augmented with:

  • Error-Detection Code: Assures that no bits have been altered in transit (data integrity).
  • Sequence Number: Assures that messages arrive in proper order without omission or duplication.
  • Timestamp: Assures that the message has not been delayed beyond normal transit limits (replay attack prevention).

Three Situations Where Auth Without Encryption is Preferable (Slide 24)

1
Multi-Destination Broadcast

When broadcasting a message to numerous destinations (e.g., an announcement of network downtime or a control center alarm), it is cheaper and more reliable for only one central monitor to verify authenticity. Plaintext broadcast with an authentication tag allows instant reading, with an alarm raised if the monitor detects forgery.

2
Heavy-Load Receivers

When a receiving node is heavily overloaded with network traffic, it cannot afford the substantial processor cycles required to decrypt every incoming message. Authentication tags allow selective, randomized verification of sample packets without encrypting bulk payloads.

3
Computer Program Execution

Executable software programs can be stored and executed directly in plaintext without wasting CPU resources decrypting them on every launch. An attached authentication tag allows integrity verification on-demand whenever assurance is required.

MAC Architecture

Message Authentication Code (MAC) (Figure 2.3 & Slide 26)

A Message Authentication Code (MAC) accepts a variable-length message $M$ and a shared secret key $K$, and outputs a small, fixed-size authentication tag:

$$ ext{MAC} = C(K, M)$$
Operational Characteristics
  • Keyed Primitive: Unlike hash functions, a MAC requires an input secret key shared between sender and receiver.
  • Non-Reversible: The MAC algorithm does NOT need to be invertible (unlike decryption ciphers).
  • Collision Resistance: Because messages have arbitrary length and the MAC is a small fixed size, many messages map to the same MAC; however, it must be computationally infeasible in practice to find pairs of messages yielding the same MAC tag.
  • Generation: Common constructions use block ciphers (e.g., DES or AES in CBC-MAC mode), using final ciphertext bits as the code.
Verification Flow
  1. Sender generates $ ext{MAC} = C(K, M)$ and transmits $M \mathbin{\Vert} ext{MAC}$.
  2. Receiver accepts $M'$, computes local $ ext{MAC}' = C(K, M')$.
  3. Receiver compares $ ext{MAC}'$ with transmitted $ ext{MAC}$. If equal, receiver is assured that the message is authentic and unaltered.

Cryptographic Hash

One-Way Hash Functions & The 3 Properties (Figure 2.4)

A one-way hash function $H$ accepts a variable-size message $M$ as input and produces a fixed-size message digest $h = H(M)$ (Figure 2.4 / Slide 27–28). Unlike a MAC, a hash function takes no secret key as input.

Padding and Length Field Structure (Slide 27 & 29)
To accommodate variable-length inputs, the message is padded to an integer multiple of a fixed block size (e.g., 512 or 1024 bits). The padding explicitly includes the bit length of the original message ($L$). This length field is a crucial security barrier that exponentially increases the difficulty for an adversary to craft an alternative message with an identical hash.

The Three Mandatory Hash Properties (Slide 32)

Property 1

Preimage Resistance (One-Way Property)

For any given hash value $h$, it is computationally infeasible to find any message $y$ such that $H(y) = h$.

Slide Protection: Protects against an adversary who only possesses an intercepted hash value $h$ and attempts to recover the original input.
Property 2

Second Preimage Resistance (Weak Collision Resistance)

For any given input block $x$, it is computationally infeasible to find a distinct block $y eq x$ such that $H(y) = H(x)$.

Slide Protection: Protects against an attacker who has an input value and its valid hash, and attempts to substitute a malicious alternative in its place.
Property 3

Collision Resistance (Strong Collision Resistance)

It is computationally infeasible to find ANY arbitrary pair $(x, y)$ with $x eq y$ such that $H(x) = H(y)$.

Slide Protection: Prevents an attacker from generating two different documents from scratch that produce an identical hash digest (birthday attacks).
Authentication Schemes

Three Hash Authentication Schemes (Figure 2.5 & Slides 31, 33, 34)

A bare hash value does not verify identity without a cryptographic binding. Figure 2.5 establishes three standard authentication architectures:

Scheme (a)

Using Symmetric Encryption

The hash code $H(M)$ is encrypted using a shared symmetric secret key $K$:

Transmit: M || E(K, H(M))
Receiver decrypts hash using $K$, recomputes $H(M)$, and compares.
Because only sender and receiver possess $K$, message authenticity is assured.
Scheme (b)

Using Public-Key (Digital Signature)

The hash code $H(M)$ is encrypted using the sender's private key ($PR_a$):

Transmit: M || E(PRa, H(M))
Receiver decrypts signature using sender's public key $PU_a$.
Two Huge Advantages (Slide 34): Provides a digital signature with nonrepudiation, and eliminates the need for pre-shared symmetric key distribution.
Scheme (c)

Using a Shared Secret Value

Sender and receiver share a secret value $S$. Hash is computed over message concatenated with secret:

Transmit: M || H(M || S)
The secret value $S$ is NEVER sent over the network!
Receiver appends its copy of $S$ to received $M$ and checks whether $H(M' \mathbin{\Vert} S) == h$. Forms the basis of HMAC.

Other Practical Applications of Hash Functions (Slide 35)

1. File System Intrusion Detection

Compute and store the hash value $H(F)$ for every system executable and configuration file $F$ on secure write-locked or write-once optical media. The system periodically scans and recomputes $H(F)$ to detect unauthorized modification or trojan insertion.

Required Property: Weak Second Preimage Resistance (an attacker must not be able to modify file $F$ into $F'$ such that $H(F') = H(F)$).

2. Operating System Password Storage

The operating system never stores plaintext passwords; instead, it stores the one-way hash $H( ext{Password})$ (often with a salt). When a user enters a credential, the system hashes it and verifies against the stored hash digest. If the shadow password file is leaked, the actual passwords cannot be reversed.

Required Property: Preimage Resistance (and second preimage resistance).

Quick Revision: Authentication & Hash Functions

High-Yield Distinctions
  • MAC vs Hash: MAC takes a secret key as input; Hash function takes NO key.
  • Invertibility: Neither MAC nor Hash algorithms require mathematical reversibility.
  • Preimage Resistance: Given $h$, find $x$.
  • Second Preimage Resistance: Given $x$, find $y eq x$ with $H(y)=H(x)$ (needed for intrusion detection).
  • Collision Resistance: Find any pair $(x, y)$ with $H(x)=H(y)$.
  • Scheme (c): Secret value $S$ is concatenated before hashing and is NEVER sent over the wire.
Instant Self-Test

Q: Why does encrypting a message hash with a sender's private key provide nonrepudiation?

A: Because only the sender possesses their unique private key. If the hash successfully decrypts using the sender's public key, only the sender could have created the signature, preventing them from denying authorship!

Asymmetric Paradigm

Public-Key Cryptography (Diffie & Hellman, 1976)

First proposed publicly by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976 [DIFF76], public-key cryptography represents the first truly revolutionary advance in cryptography in thousands of years. Unlike symmetric ciphers which rely on bit permutations and substitutions, public-key algorithms are founded on asymmetric mathematical functions utilizing two distinct keys: a Public Key ($PU$) and a Private Key ($PR$).

Three Common Misconceptions Refuted (Slide 37)

Misconception 1

"Public-key is more secure than symmetric encryption."

FALSE

The security of any encryption scheme depends solely on (1) the key length and (2) the computational work required to break the cipher. There is nothing in principle that makes public-key inherently more resistant to cryptanalysis than symmetric ciphers.

Misconception 2

"Public-key has made symmetric encryption obsolete."

FALSE

Due to the enormous computational overhead of asymmetric number-theoretic modular arithmetic, public-key ciphers are roughly 1,000 times slower than symmetric ciphers. Symmetric ciphers remain essential for high-speed bulk data encryption.

Misconception 3

"Key distribution is trivial in public-key systems."

FALSE

Distributing public keys requires trusted certification protocols and central authorities (CAs) to prevent forgery and impersonation. The procedures are neither simpler nor more efficient than symmetric key distribution centers (KDCs).

Dual Operation

Confidentiality vs. Authentication in Public-Key Cryptography (Figure 2.6)

The asymmetric key pair can be deployed in two distinct configurations depending on the security objective (Slides 38–39):

Mode (a): Confidentiality

Encryption with Receiver's Public Key

Sender: C = E(PU_Bob, M)
Receiver: M = D(PR_Bob, C)
  • Sender encrypts using the recipient's public key ($PU_b$).
  • Because only the intended recipient possesses the matching private key ($PR_b$), only they can decrypt the message.
  • Target Objective: Provides strict Confidentiality (secrecy of message content).
Mode (b): Authentication

Encryption with Sender's Private Key

Sender: C = E(PR_Alice, M)
Receiver: M = D(PU_Alice, C)
  • Sender encrypts using their own private key ($PR_a$).
  • Anyone possessing the sender's public key ($PU_a$) can decrypt and read the message.
  • Target Objective: Provides Authentication, Data Integrity & Digital Signature (proves only Alice could have originated the message).

Formal Requirements

The 5 Conditions of Diffie-Hellman (Slide 41)

In 1976, Diffie and Hellman formulated the five mathematical conditions an asymmetric cryptosystem must fulfill:

  1. Easy Key Pair Generation: It is computationally easy for a party $B$ to generate a key pair consisting of public key $PU_b$ and private key $PR_b$.
  2. Easy Encryption: It is computationally easy for a sender $A$, knowing the public key $PU_b$ and message $M$, to generate the ciphertext: $C = E(PU_b, M)$.
  3. Easy Decryption: It is computationally easy for receiver $B$ to decrypt $C$ using their private key $PR_b$ to recover the original message: $M = D(PR_b, C) = D[PR_b, E(PU_b, M)]$.
  4. Infeasible Key Recovery: It is computationally infeasible for an opponent, knowing the public key $PU_b$, to determine the private key $PR_b$.
  5. Infeasible Plaintext Recovery: It is computationally infeasible for an opponent, knowing the public key $PU_b$ and a ciphertext $C$, to recover the original message $M$.

The RSA Algorithm (RIVE78 / Slide 42)

Developed in 1977 by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman at MIT (published 1978), RSA is a block cipher in which plaintext and ciphertext are integers between $0$ and $n-1$ for some integer $n$.

FIPS Standard

Digital Signatures (FIPS PUB 186-4 DSS)

NIST FIPS PUB 186-4 (Digital Signature Standard (DSS), July 2013) establishes the authoritative definition:

FIPS 186-4 Definition
"The result of a cryptographic transformation of data that, when properly implemented, provides a mechanism for verifying origin authentication, data integrity and signatory non-repudiation."

A digital signature is a data-dependent bit pattern generated as a function of a message block. A verifier checks:

  1. The data block has been signed by the alleged signer.
  2. The data block has not been altered since signing.
  3. The signer cannot repudiate the signature.

1. Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA)

The original NIST-approved algorithm; based on the computational difficulty of calculating discrete logarithms.

2. RSA Digital Signature Algorithm

Based on the mathematical structure of the RSA public-key algorithm.

3. ECDSA

Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm; based on elliptic-curve cryptography.

Key Management

Public-Key Certificates & The Certificate Authority (Figure 2.8 & Slide 6–8)

The Impersonation Vulnerability (Week 4 Slide 6)

The Public Announcement Vulnerability
"Anyone can forge such a public announcement. That is, some user could pretend to be Bob and send a public key to another participant or broadcast such a public key. Until such time as Bob discovers the forgery and alerts other participants, the forger is able to read all encrypted messages intended for Bob and can use the forged keys for authentication."

Public-Key Certificate Architecture (Figure 2.8)

To thwart forgery, a trusted third party known as a Certificate Authority (CA) binds a user's identity to their public key:

  • Unsigned Certificate: Contains user ID (Bob's identity), Bob's public key, and information concerning the CA.
  • CA Signature: The CA generates a hash code of the unsigned certificate and encrypts the hash using the CA's private key ($PR_{CA}$).
  • Signed Certificate: The unsigned certificate combined with the CA's digital signature forms the complete certificate.

The Complete 8-Step Verification Workflow (Week 4 Slide 8)

1 Client generates public/private key pair.
2 Client presents public key to CA with verified proof of identity.
3 CA validates identity credentials.
4 CA constructs unsigned certificate with client ID and public key.
5 CA signs certificate by encrypting its hash with CA's private key ($PR_{CA}$).
6 CA returns the signed certificate to client.
7 Client may provide the signed certificate to any other user (e.g., Alice).
8 Any user may verify that the certificate is valid as follows:
    a. User calculates the hash code of the certificate (not including the signature).
    b. User verifies the digital signature using the CA's public key and the signature verification algorithm, returning a result of either signature valid or invalid.

Preoutput: LD₀ = RE₁₆, RD₀ = LE₁₆

• LD₁ = RD₀ = LE₁₆ = RE₁₅

4. DES 28-bit Key Restoration

Key split into two 28-bit halves (C₀, D₀).
• Rounds 1, 2, 9, 16 shift by 1 bit: 4 × 1 = 4 bits
• Other 12 rounds shift by 2 bits: 12 × 2 = 24 bits
• Total circular shift = 4 + 24 = 28 bits.
• 28 mod 28 = 0 → Halves return to original position!
